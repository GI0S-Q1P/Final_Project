discover things about Uranus

Main information

Uranus is the seventh planet from the Sun. It is a gaseous cyan-coloured ice giant. Most of the planet is made of water, ammonia, and methane in a supercritical phase of matter, which astronomy calls "ice" or volatiles. The planet's atmosphere has a complex layered cloud structure and has the lowest minimum temperature (49 K (−224 °C; −371 °F)) of all the Solar System's planets. It has a marked axial tilt of 82.23° with a retrograde rotation period of 17 hours and 14 minutes. This means that in an 84-Earth-year orbital period around the Sun, its poles experience about 42 years of continuous sunlight followed by 42 years of darkness.

Uranus has the third-largest diameter and fourth-largest mass among the Solar System's planets. Based on current models, beneath its volatile mantle layer lies a rocky core, surrounded by a thick hydrogen and helium atmosphere. Trace amounts of hydrocarbons (likely produced through chemical processes in the atmosphere) and carbon monoxide along with carbon dioxide (thought to have originated from comets) have been detected in the upper atmosphere. Uranus also shows unusual atmospheric behaviour, including peak wind speeds of up to 900 km/h (560 mph), variations in its polar regions, and irregular cloud activity. It also has very low internal heat compared to other giant planets, and the reason for this remains unclear.

Like the other giant planets, Uranus has a ring system, a magnetosphere, and many natural satellites. Its rings are extremely dark, reflecting only about 2% of incoming sunlight. Uranus has 29 known moons, including 19 regular moons and five major ones: Miranda, Ariel, Umbriel, Titania, and Oberon. Ten additional irregular moons orbit farther out. Its magnetosphere is highly asymmetric and filled with charged particles, which may contribute to the darkening of its rings and moons.

Uranus is technically visible to the naked eye, but it is extremely dim and moves very slowly against background stars, so it was not recognized as a planet until 1781 when William Herschel observed it. The planet is named after the Greek god Uranus (Ouranos). As of 2026, it has been visited only once by Voyager 2 in 1986. It remains a major target for future missions, including proposed orbiters and probes prioritized in recent planetary science surveys and mission concepts.

Formation

It is argued that the differences between the ice giants and the gas giants arise from their formation history. The Solar System is hypothesised to have formed from a rotating disk of gas and dust known as the presolar nebula. Much of the nebula's gas, primarily hydrogen and helium, formed the Sun, and the dust grains collected together to form the first protoplanets. As the planets grew, some of them eventually accreted enough matter for their gravity to hold on to the nebula's leftover gas. As they held onto more gas, they grew larger; and as they grew larger, they attracted even more gas until a critical point was reached, after which their growth increased rapidly.

The ice giants, with only a few Earth masses of nebular gas, never reached that runaway growth threshold.

Recent simulations of planetary migration suggest that both ice giants may have formed closer to the Sun than their current positions and later moved outward after formation (the Nice model).

- - - Rotation - - -

Uranus orbits the Sun once every 84 years. As viewed against the background of stars, since being discovered in 1781, the planet has returned to the point of its discovery northeast of the binary star Zeta Tauri twice—in March 1865 and March 1949—and will return to this location again in April 2033.

Because the orbital period is very close to 84 years, Uranus appears in nearly the same position in the sky every 84 years. Its retrograde motion also causes it to drift eastward each year, returning to roughly the same apparent position relative to background stars. Its average distance from the Sun is about 20 AU (3 billion km), and the difference between its closest and farthest points in orbit is 1.8 AU, larger than any planet except Pluto. Because sunlight decreases with distance squared, Uranus receives only about 1/400 of the sunlight that Earth receives.

The planet’s orbit was first calculated in 1783 by Pierre-Simon Laplace. Later discrepancies between predicted and observed positions led John Couch Adams and Urbain Le Verrier to independently propose the existence of another planet influencing Uranus. This led to the discovery of Neptune in 1846 by Johann Galle near the predicted position.

The rotational period of Uranus’s interior is about 17 hours, 14 minutes, and 52 seconds, determined by tracking auroral motion. Like other giant planets, its upper atmosphere has strong winds in the direction of rotation. At some latitudes, especially near 60° south, atmospheric features can rotate much faster, completing a full turn in as little as 14 hours.

- - - The core - - -

Uranus's mass is roughly 14.5 times that of Earth, making it the least massive of the giant planets. Its diameter is slightly larger than Neptune's at roughly four times that of Earth. A resulting density of 1.27 g/cm³ makes Uranus the second least dense planet, after Saturn. This value indicates that it is made primarily of various ices, such as water, ammonia, and methane. The total mass of ice in Uranus's interior is not precisely known, because different figures emerge depending on the model chosen; it must be between 9.3 and 13.5 Earth masses. Hydrogen and helium constitute only a small part of the total, with between 0.5 and 1.5 Earth masses. The remainder of the non-ice mass (0.5 to 3.7 Earth masses) is accounted for by rocky material.

The standard model of Uranus's structure is that it consists of three layers: a rocky (silicate/iron–nickel) core in the centre, an icy mantle in the middle, and an outer gaseous hydrogen/helium envelope. The core is relatively small, with a mass of only 0.55 Earth masses and a radius less than 20% of the planet; the mantle comprises its bulk, with around 13.4 Earth masses, and the upper atmosphere is relatively insubstantial, weighing about 0.5 Earth masses and extending for the last 20% of Uranus's radius. Uranus's core density is around 9 g/cm³, with a pressure in the centre of 8 million bars (800 GPa) and a temperature of about 5000 K. The ice mantle is not in fact composed of ice in the conventional sense, but of a hot and dense fluid consisting of water, ammonia and other volatiles. This fluid, which has a high electrical conductivity, is sometimes called a water–ammonia ocean.
