discover things about Jupiter

Main information

Jupiter is the fifth planet from the Sun, and the largest in the Solar System. It is a gas giant with a mass nearly 2.5 times that of all the other planets in the Solar System combined and slightly less than one-thousandth the mass of the Sun. Its diameter is 11 times that of Earth and a tenth that of the Sun. Jupiter orbits the Sun at a distance of 5.20 AU (778.5 Gm), with an orbital period of 11.86 years. It is the third-brightest natural object in Earth's night sky, after the Moon and Venus, and has been observed since prehistoric times. Its name comes from Jupiter, the chief deity of ancient Roman religion.

Jupiter was the first of the Sun's planets to form, and its inward migration during the early Solar System influenced the formation history of the other planets. Jupiter's atmosphere consists of 76% hydrogen and 24% helium by mass, with a denser interior. It also contains traces of carbon, oxygen, sulfur, neon, and compounds such as ammonia, water vapour, phosphine, hydrogen sulfide, and hydrocarbons. Jupiter's helium abundance is about 80% of the Sun's, similar to Saturn's composition.

The outer atmosphere is divided into a series of latitudinal bands, with turbulence and storms along their boundaries. The most famous feature is the Great Red Spot, a giant storm that has been observed since at least 1831.

Because of Jupiter's rapid rotation rate, completing one turn in about ten hours, the planet is shaped as an oblate spheroid. Its equatorial radius is about 7% larger than its polar radius. Jupiter's internal structure is believed to include an outer mantle of fluid metallic hydrogen and a diffuse inner core of denser material. The planet also emits more heat than it receives from the Sun due to ongoing contraction within its interior.

Jupiter's magnetic field is the strongest and second-largest continuous structure in the Solar System. It is generated by electrical currents within the fluid metallic hydrogen layer. The solar wind interacts with this magnetosphere, stretching it outward and influencing the surrounding space environment.

At least 115 moons orbit Jupiter. The four largest—Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto—are known as the Galilean moons and can be seen with binoculars. Ganymede is the largest moon in the Solar System and is even larger than Mercury.

Jupiter also has a faint ring system made mainly of dust. The rings include an inner halo, a brighter main ring, and an outer gossamer ring. They appear reddish in visible and near-infrared light. The age of the ring system is uncertain and may date back to Jupiter's formation.

Since 1973, Jupiter has been explored by nine robotic space probes, including flybys and orbiters, with additional missions currently on the way. Jupiter-like exoplanets have also been discovered around other stars.

General characteristics

Jupiter is a gas giant, meaning its chemical composition is primarily hydrogen and helium. These materials are classified as gases in planetary geology, though the term does not necessarily describe their physical state. It is the largest planet in the Solar System, with a diameter of 142,976 km (88,841 mi) at its equator, giving it a volume 1,321 times that of Earth.

Its average density is 1.326 g/cm³, which is lower than the densities of all four terrestrial planets.

- - - Rotation - - -

The axial tilt of Jupiter is 3.13°, which is relatively small, so its seasons are insignificant compared to those of Earth and Mars.

Jupiter's rotation is the fastest of all the Solar System's planets, completing one rotation in slightly less than ten hours. This rapid rotation creates an equatorial bulge that can be seen even with amateur telescopes. The equatorial radius is about 7% larger than the polar radius.

Because Jupiter is not a solid body, its upper atmosphere undergoes differential rotation. The atmosphere near the poles rotates about five minutes slower than the atmosphere at the equator.

Three systems are used as reference frames for tracking Jupiter's rotation and atmospheric motion. System I applies to latitudes from 7° N to 7° S and has a rotation period of 9h 50m 30.0s, making it the shortest. System II applies to higher latitudes north and south and has a period of 9h 55m 40.6s. System III corresponds to the rotation of Jupiter's magnetosphere and is considered the planet's official rotation period.

- - - The core - - -

Before the early 21st century, most scientists proposed one of two scenarios for the formation of Jupiter. If the planet formed first as a solid body, it would contain a dense core surrounded by fluid metallic hydrogen and an outer atmosphere mostly made of molecular hydrogen. Alternatively, if Jupiter collapsed directly from the gaseous protoplanetary disk, it would likely lack a solid core entirely, becoming denser toward the centre through layers of hydrogen.

Data from NASA's Juno mission showed that Jupiter actually has a diffuse core mixed into its mantle, extending across roughly 30–50% of the planet’s radius. This core may contain heavy elements with a combined mass 7–25 times that of Earth. Scientists think this mixed structure may have formed either during Jupiter’s early growth as it collected gas and solids, or from a massive collision with a young planet about ten times Earth’s mass shortly after Jupiter formed.

Outside the metallic hydrogen layer lies a transparent interior atmosphere of hydrogen. At these depths, the pressure and temperature exceed hydrogen’s critical point, meaning there is no clear distinction between liquid and gas. Instead, hydrogen exists as a supercritical fluid. Deeper inside Jupiter, hydrogen and helium gradually transition into liquid-like layers resembling an ocean of liquid hydrogen and other supercritical materials.

As depth increases, the gas becomes hotter and denser. Helium and neon can condense into droplets and rain downward through the atmosphere, reducing their abundance in the upper layers. Scientists also believe diamond rain may occur inside Jupiter, similar to theories about Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune.

Temperature and pressure rise steadily toward Jupiter's center because heat from the planet’s formation escapes mainly through convection. Near the visible cloud tops, where pressure is similar to Earth’s atmospheric pressure, temperatures are around 165 K (−108 °C; −163 °F). Deeper regions where hydrogen transitions into metallic fluid experience pressures between 50–400 GPa and temperatures between 5,000–8,400 K. Jupiter’s diluted core may reach temperatures of about 20,000 K (19,700 °C; 35,500 °F) under pressures near 4,000 GPa.
