discover things about Neptune

Main information

Neptune is the eighth and farthest known planet orbiting the Sun. It is the fourth-largest planet in the Solar System by diameter, the third-most-massive planet, and the densest giant planet. It is 17 times the mass of Earth. Compared to Uranus, its neighbouring ice giant, Neptune is slightly smaller, but more massive and dense. Being composed primarily of gases and liquids, it has no well-defined solid surface. Neptune orbits the Sun once every 164.8 years at an orbital distance of 30.1 astronomical units (4.5 billion kilometres; 2.8 billion miles). It is named after the Roman god of the sea and has the astronomical symbol ♆, representing Neptune's trident.

Neptune is not visible to the unaided eye and is the only planet in the Solar System that was not initially observed through direct empirical observation. Instead, unexpected changes in the orbit of Uranus led Alexis Bouvard to hypothesise that another unseen planet was affecting it. After Bouvard's death, the position of Neptune was mathematically predicted independently by John Couch Adams and Urbain Le Verrier. Neptune was later observed on 23 September 1846 by Johann Gottfried Galle within a degree of Le Verrier’s predicted position. Its largest moon, Triton, was discovered shortly afterward, while the rest of its moons were identified only in the 20th century.

Neptune's great distance from Earth makes it appear very small and dim, making detailed observation difficult from Earth-based telescopes. Only modern instruments such as the Hubble Space Telescope and large ground-based telescopes with adaptive optics have allowed detailed study. Voyager 2, which flew past Neptune on 25 August 1989, remains the only spacecraft to have visited the planet. Like Jupiter and Saturn, Neptune’s atmosphere is mostly hydrogen and helium, with traces of hydrocarbons and possibly nitrogen, but it contains a higher proportion of ices such as water, ammonia, and methane. These, combined with Rayleigh scattering, give Neptune its faint blue colour.

Unlike Uranus, which can appear relatively featureless for long periods, Neptune has active and persistent weather systems. At the time of Voyager 2’s flyby, a large dark storm system was observed, similar in appearance to Jupiter’s Great Red Spot. Later observations revealed additional dark storms. Neptune has the strongest sustained winds in the Solar System, reaching speeds of up to 2,100 km/h. Because it is so far from the Sun, its cloud tops are extremely cold at around 55 K (−218 °C), while its core is estimated to reach about 5,400 K. Neptune also has a faint, fragmented ring system known as arcs, first discovered in 1984 and confirmed by Voyager 2.

General characteristics

Neptune's mass of 1.024x10^26 kg is intermediate between Earth and the larger gas giants: it is 17.15 times that of Earth but just 1/19th that of Jupiter. Its gravity at 1 bar is 11.27 m/s², 1.15 times the surface gravity of Earth, and surpassed only by Jupiter. Neptune's equatorial radius of 24,764 km is nearly four times that of Earth. Neptune, like Uranus, is an ice giant, a subclass of giant planet, because they are smaller and have higher concentrations of volatiles than Jupiter and Saturn.

In the search for exoplanets, Neptune has been used as a metonym: discovered bodies of similar mass are often referred to as "Neptunes", just as scientists refer to various extrasolar bodies as "Jupiters".

- - - Rotation - - -

The average distance between Neptune and the Sun is 4.5 billion km (about 30.1 astronomical units (AU), the mean distance from the Earth to the Sun), and it completes an orbit on average every 164.79 years, subject to a variability of around ±0.1 years. The perihelion distance is 29.81 AU, and the aphelion distance is 30.33 AU. Neptune's orbital eccentricity is only 0.008678, making it the planet in the Solar System with the second most circular orbit after Venus. The orbit of Neptune is inclined 1.77° compared to that of Earth.

On 11 July 2011, Neptune completed its first full barycentric orbit since its discovery in 1846; it did not appear at its exact discovery position in the sky because Earth was in a different location in its 365.26-day orbit. Because of the motion of the Sun in relation to the barycentre of the Solar System, on 11 July, Neptune was not at its exact discovery position in relation to the Sun—if the more common heliocentric coordinate system is used, the discovery longitude was reached on 12 July 2011.

- - - The core - - -

Neptune's internal structure resembles that of Uranus. Its atmosphere forms about 5 to 10% of its mass and extends perhaps 10 to 20% of the way towards the core. Pressure in the atmosphere reaches about 10 GPa, or about 105 atmospheres. Increasing concentrations of methane, ammonia, and water are found in the lower regions of the atmosphere.

The mantle is equivalent to 10 to 15 Earth masses and is rich in water, ammonia, and methane. As is customary in planetary science, this mixture is called "icy" even though it is a hot, dense supercritical fluid. This fluid, which has high electrical conductivity, is sometimes called a water–ammonia ocean. The mantle may include a layer of ionic water in which water molecules break into a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen ions, and deeper still a superionic phase where oxygen forms a crystalline lattice and hydrogen ions move freely through it. At depths around 7,000 km, methane may decompose into diamond crystals that fall inward like hail. Scientists think similar diamond rain may occur on Jupiter, Saturn, and Uranus. Some experiments even suggest parts of the mantle could behave like a liquid carbon ocean with solid diamond structures floating within it.

The core of Neptune is likely composed of iron, nickel, and silicates, with a mass around 1.2 times that of Earth. The pressure at the centre is about 7 Mbar (700 GPa), roughly twice that of Earth’s core, and the temperature may reach around 5,400 K.
